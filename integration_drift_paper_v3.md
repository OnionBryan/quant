# INTEGRATION DRIFT: INSTITUTIONAL CONSTRAINTS AND HR TECHNOLOGY SYSTEM INTEGRATION IN THE PUBLIC SECTOR

**Peter Demerjian, Bryan Gibson, Brandon Hall**
Working Paper - December 2025

---

## ABSTRACT

This study examines why public sector organizations systematically fail to achieve planned legacy system retirement following HR technology acquisitions. We introduce Integration Drift—the divergence between planned and actual system integration outcomes caused by institutional constraints—and develop the Institutional-Technical-Organizational (ITO) Attribution Framework to decompose delay into addressable components. Using comparative case analysis of the U.S. Army's Integrated Personnel and Pay System-Army (IPPS-A), OPM's forthcoming government-wide HCM initiative, and Walmart's Workday implementation, we examine institutional amplification: whether and how constraints function not merely as additive delays but as multipliers on technical and organizational challenges.

IPPS-A demonstrates measurable Integration Drift: the project planned Release 3 deployment by December 2021 but achieved it in January 2023 (13-month delay), with payroll integration deferred from 2023 to calendar year 2026 (36-month deferral). These documented delays correlate temporally with institutional milestones: Federal Acquisition Regulation (FAR) testing requirements (announced October 2021), appropriations constraints, and civil service change management. In contrast, Walmart's Workday implementation across 2.1 million employees achieved full legacy system retirement within planned timelines, despite broadly comparable technical complexity.

Using comparative case analysis with ITO attribution coding, we find evidence that institutional constraints do not merely add to project timelines but amplify technical and organizational delays through sequential requirements, constraint cascades, and risk-averse responses. We ground this amplification mechanism in a unified framework (Quigley, Tsebelis, Fukuyama, Kornai) explaining why constraints persist and why public organizations lack survival pressure to overcome them. The paper offers testable predictions for OPM's forthcoming implementation.

**Keywords:** Integration drift, institutional theory, veto players, vetocracy, soft budget constraints, HRIS, public sector IT, M&A integration

## 1. INTRODUCTION

### 1.1 The Empirical Puzzle

In fiscal year 2024, the U.S. federal government requested approximately $74 billion for civilian agency information technology, with additional billions allocated to defense IT systems (OMB, 2024). HR and personnel systems modernization represents a substantial portion of this investment. Despite this investment, federal IT projects consistently exceed planned timelines and fail to achieve targeted legacy system retirement rates. The Government Accountability Office has designated federal IT acquisition and management as "high risk" since 2015, documenting persistent patterns of schedule slippage, cost overruns, and incomplete system consolidation (GAO, 2024).

The financial magnitude of these patterns warrants attention. Federal agencies spend more than $100 billion annually on IT investments, with approximately 80 percent—roughly $80 billion—allocated to operations and maintenance of existing systems, including legacy infrastructure (GAO, 2023). GAO analysis of just ten critical federal legacy systems found annual maintenance costs of $337 million (GAO, 2019). From fiscal years 2010 to 2017, operations and maintenance spending increases produced a $7.3 billion decline in funds available for development, modernization, and enhancement activities (GAO, 2016). Each month of Integration Drift extends legacy maintenance burdens while deferring modernization benefits—a compounding cost structure whose net present value accounting analysis could quantify.

The standard explanation attributes these outcomes to poor management, inadequate planning, or insufficient technical expertise. This explanation is unsatisfying for three reasons. First, it fails to explain why the pattern persists across agencies with different leadership, different contractors, and different technical requirements. If poor management were the cause, variation would be the expectation. Instead, we observe convergence toward similar (unfavorable) outcomes. Second, it implies that private sector organizations implementing equivalent systems are simply better managed—an assumption that ignores the fundamentally different institutional environments in which public and private organizations operate. Third, it suggests no actionable remedy beyond exhortation to "do better."

This paper examines post-acquisition integration of large-scale HR technology platforms in public versus private settings, advancing an alternative explanation grounded in institutional theory. We argue that public sector IT projects are not poorly managed but differently constrained. The rules governing federal acquisitions—Federal Acquisition Regulation (FAR) protest periods, congressional appropriations cycles, civil service protections, collective bargaining agreements—exist to ensure democratic accountability, fair competition, and worker protection. These are better understood as intentional features of public governance rather than as bureaucratic pathologies. However, these features impose costs that compound in ways existing literature has not adequately theorized.

### 1.2 Integration Drift: Concept and Definition

We introduce Integration Drift as a theoretical construct capturing involuntary divergence between planned and actual system integration outcomes:

    DEFINITION: Integration Drift is the gap between an organization's
    stated integration intent at acquisition announcement (T0) and actual
    system preservation at post-implementation assessment (T0 + 24 months),
    where the gap emerges from institutional constraints the organization
    cannot unilaterally change.

Three elements distinguish Integration Drift from general project delay. First, it is measured as outcome divergence (system preservation rate) rather than timeline extension. Second, it is involuntary—organizations experiencing drift intended full integration but were prevented from achieving it. Third, it is institutional—the constraints causing drift are embedded in law, regulation, or collective agreement, not organizational choice or technical limitation alone.

The term "drift" is used to emphasize continuous, cumulative divergence from intended trajectory—unlike "failure" (which implies culpable inadequacy) or "delay" (which implies eventual achievement).

### 1.3 Research Questions

This study addresses three interrelated questions:

    RQ1: Do public sector organizations exhibit systematically higher
    Integration Drift than private sector organizations implementing
    equivalent HR technology?

    RQ2: What is the relative contribution of institutional, technical,
    and organizational factors to observed Integration Drift?

    RQ3: Do institutional constraints operate as additive delays or as
    amplifiers of technical and organizational challenges?

### 1.4 Preview of Findings

Comparative analysis of Army IPPS-A and Walmart Workday implementations reveals substantial sector differences. IPPS-A, targeting consolidation of approximately 50 legacy systems serving 1.1 million soldiers, has demonstrated Integration Drift: the project planned Release 3 deployment by December 2021 but achieved it in January 2023 (13-month delay), with payroll integration deferred from 2023 to calendar year 2026 (36-month deferral).

In contrast, Walmart implemented Workday across 2.1 million employees globally, achieving complete legacy system retirement within planned timelines. Technical complexity in both migrations appears broadly comparable; the divergence in outcomes is most consistent with differences in institutional context rather than technical factors alone.

### 1.5 Contributions

This study makes three contributions. Theoretically, we extend M&A integration research by grounding Integration Drift in a unified framework spanning Quigley, Tsebelis, Fukuyama, and Kornai—explaining how institutional constraints accumulate, resist reform, and why organizations lack pressure to overcome them. Methodologically, we develop the ITO Attribution Framework, a replicable approach for decomposing project delay into addressable components. Practically, we distinguish delays amenable to management improvement from those requiring institutional reform.

## 2. THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK

### 2.1 M&A Integration Theory and Its Limits

The M&A literature conceptualizes post-acquisition integration as strategic choice. Haspeslagh and Jemison (1991) identify four integration approaches—absorption, preservation, symbiosis, and holding—distinguished by strategic interdependence needs and organizational autonomy requirements. The implicit assumption: integration mode is chosen through deliberate decision-making.

We challenge this assumption in institutionally constrained environments. Organizations may intend absorption but achieve preservation—not through choice but through institutional prevention. Integration Drift captures this involuntary divergence between strategy and outcome.

This matters because incomplete integration is a primary driver of M&A value destruction. King et al. (2004) find in their meta-analysis that post-acquisition performance depends significantly on integration effectiveness. When HRIS integration stalls, organizations bear perpetual dual-system maintenance costs and fail to realize anticipated synergies. Integration Drift thus represents a specific channel through which institutional constraints translate into unrealized value—not merely delay, but ongoing value leakage for as long as legacy systems persist.

### 2.2 Public Sector IT: Institutional Differences

Research documents systematic differences between public and private sector IT implementation. Moon and Bretschneider (2002) find that perceived red tape significantly constrains IT innovativeness in public organizations. Fountain (2001) argues that identical technologies produce different outcomes when enacted through different institutional arrangements. Troshani, Jerram, and Hill (2011) find that public sector HRIS adoption follows different patterns than private sector adoption, with longer timelines and more stakeholder involvement.

What this literature does not fully theorize is the mechanism by which institutional constraints produce specific outcomes. We turn to a novel theoretical synthesis.

### 2.3 From Quigley to Kornai: A Unified Framework

We propose a unified framework explaining why institutional constraints persist, accumulate, amplify project timelines—and why organizations lack survival pressure to overcome them. This framework spans four theoretical contributions: Quigley's instrument/institution distinction, Tsebelis's veto player theory, Fukuyama's concept of vetocracy, and Kornai's soft budget constraint theory.

#### 2.3.1 Instruments and Institutions (Quigley, 1961)

Carroll Quigley's foundational insight distinguishes between instruments and institutions. An instrument is an organization that does what it was designed to do—it serves a genuine social need and little else. Over time, however, instruments transform into institutions: they "take on a life of their own, protect themselves and serve their own interests more than they advance the cultural need they were designed to solve" (Quigley, 1961: 101).

This transformation is not conspiracy or corruption. The procurement officer who masters FAR complexity has invested years in that expertise; simplifying FAR threatens that investment. The contractor who has learned to navigate federal acquisition has built competitive advantage on that knowledge. None of these actors are villains; each can be viewed as rationally protecting accumulated expertise and interests. Arthur (1994) and Pierson (2000) formalize this mechanism as increasing returns: once actors invest in mastering a complex system, switching costs rise over time, creating path dependence that makes reform progressively more difficult. For M&A integration, this implies that acquirers inherit not just legacy systems but the accumulated institutional investments protecting those systems—a burden private acquirers can override but public sector organizations often cannot.

Applied to federal IT acquisition: FAR began as an instrument to prevent procurement corruption. It worked. But FAR has become an institution—over 2,000 pages of accumulated complexity serving an ecosystem of lawyers, consultants, and procurement officials whose livelihoods depend on that complexity. Therefore we expect many institutional constraints to persist even when they are widely recognized as imposing costs.

#### 2.3.2 Veto Players and Institutional Gridlock (Tsebelis, 2002)

George Tsebelis's veto players theory explains why institutions resist reform. A veto player is any actor whose agreement is required for policy change. Tsebelis demonstrates formally that "having more veto players necessarily makes a polity less able to change its policies" (Tsebelis, 2002: 2).

The American constitutional system was deliberately designed with multiple veto players to prevent tyranny. But veto players have proliferated far beyond the constitutional structure:

    VETO PLAYERS IN FEDERAL IT ACQUISITION:

    Constitutional:  President, House, Senate
    Statutory:       GAO, Inspectors General, Courts, Agency CIO
    Regulatory:      OMB, OPM, GSA
    Contractual:     Unions (AFGE, NTEU), incumbent contractors
    Informal:        Congressional committees, media, interest groups

    Result: Fifteen or more actors with effective veto power over change,
            and no single actor with authority to compel completion.

Tsebelis's theory predicts the consequence: with this many veto players, "significant departures from the status quo are impossible" (Tsebelis, 2002: 19). Any major IT initiative must either satisfy all veto players (which takes years) or circumvent them (which creates legal and political risk). Therefore we expect project delay to correlate positively with the number of veto players whose approval is required.

#### 2.3.3 Vetocracy and Its Costs (Fukuyama, 2014)

Francis Fukuyama synthesizes these dynamics in his concept of vetocracy—"a system of governance whereby no single entity can acquire enough power to make decisions and take effective charge." The American system, Fukuyama argues, has evolved from healthy checks and balances into pathological vetocracy:

    "The American system was designed to prevent tyranny by making action
    difficult. It succeeded. But it provided no corresponding mechanism to
    force action when action is needed. The result: everyone can say no,
    and nobody can say yes."

Applied to federal IT acquisition: Federal agencies do not choose preservation over absorption; they are pushed toward preservation by a system in which fifteen actors can block integration and zero actors can force it. Therefore we expect federal IT projects to converge toward similar outcomes across a range of management quality and stated intent.

#### 2.3.4 Soft Budget Constraints and Asymmetric Risk (Kornai, 1986)

The preceding analysis explains how institutional constraints accumulate and resist reform. A complementary question remains: why don't organizations simply push harder to overcome these constraints? Janos Kornai's theory of soft budget constraints provides the foundation for our answer, though the mechanism in federal agencies differs from Kornai's original formulation.

Kornai, analyzing socialist economies, observed that state enterprises behaved differently than market firms because they faced fundamentally different survival conditions. Market firms operate under hard budget constraints: if revenues persistently fall short of costs, the firm fails. This creates intense pressure to eliminate inefficiencies—including the costly maintenance of redundant systems. State enterprises, by contrast, operate under soft budget constraints: if revenues fall short, the state provides subsidies. The enterprise survives regardless of efficiency.

Federal agencies do not receive explicit subsidies in the manner Kornai described; they operate within fixed appropriations rather than receiving bailouts when projects fail. However, two features of the federal environment produce behavioral effects analogous to soft budget constraints.

First, federal agencies face no competitive pressure. Unlike private firms, no competitor can capture market share by integrating faster or operating more efficiently. Walmart's delayed integration would create competitive disadvantage relative to Target, Amazon, or Costco; the Army's delayed integration creates no equivalent organizational threat. The absence of competitive pressure removes the external forcing function that drives private organizations through difficult transitions.

Second, federal managers face asymmetric risk structures. Legacy maintenance costs are diffuse, distributed across appropriations line items, and largely invisible to political principals and oversight bodies. A $50 million annual legacy maintenance cost spread across multiple budget categories attracts no attention. But transition failures are concentrated, attributable, and career-ending. A soldier not paid correctly generates a congressional inquiry. A benefit miscalculation produces an Inspector General investigation. A system outage during deployment creates a hearing.

Under these conditions, rational managers preserve legacy systems indefinitely. The financial cost of redundancy threatens no individual's career; the political cost of transition failure is immediate and personal. This asymmetric risk structure, combined with absent competitive pressure, produces the functional equivalent of Kornai's soft budget constraint: organizations lack survival pressure to overcome institutional barriers and face asymmetric personal consequences if they try aggressively and fail.

Kornai's framework thus completes the theoretical synthesis. Quigley explains how instruments transform into self-perpetuating institutions. Tsebelis demonstrates how veto player accumulation produces gridlock. Fukuyama shows how this gridlock becomes vetocracy. Kornai, adapted to the federal context, addresses the prior question: why don't organizations simply push through these barriers? The answer is that public organizations face no survival pressure to do so—and face asymmetric career risk if they try. Therefore we expect public organizations to exhibit greater tolerance for persistent inefficiencies than private organizations facing hard budget constraints and competitive pressure.

### 2.4 Institutional Amplification: How Constraints Multiply

Quigley's (1961) distinction between instruments and institutions finds a modern, rigorous parallel in Lo's (2017; Levin & Lo, 2015) analysis of regulatory evolution. Lo documents how financial regulation, initially adaptive responses to crises, accretes into increasingly complex systems whose compliance costs grow more than proportionally with the number of rules. Applied here, each new procurement statute or FAR subpart began as a targeted solution (prevent fraud, ensure competition, protect workers) but cumulatively produces compliance burdens that amplify technical and organizational delays far beyond additive expectation—precisely the mechanism we term institutional amplification.

Three specific mechanisms operate through the unified framework:

Sequential Requirements emerge from veto-driven serialization. FAR often requires sequential rather than parallel processes. Veto players at each stage can halt progress, forcing sequential approval chains. A private organization can conduct system testing while finalizing contracts, run parallel workstreams, and compress timelines through concurrent activity. A public organization must typically complete each gate before proceeding to the next: System Acceptance Testing must conclude before System Integration Testing begins; appropriations must be secured before obligations can be made; union consultation must complete before implementation can proceed. What private organizations accomplish in parallel, public organizations must accomplish in series.

Constraint Cascades emerge from institutional density. Institutional constraints interact in ways that create additional constraints. Union agreements negotiated for one change apply to subsequent changes, creating precedents that must be honored. Appropriations secured for one fiscal year constrain spending patterns in subsequent years through color-of-money restrictions and continuing resolution limitations. Security authorizations granted for one system configuration must be renewed when configurations change. Each constraint creates additional constraints—path dependence at the institutional level that compounds over project lifecycles.

Risk-Averse Responses emerge from what Kelman (1990) identifies as "fear of discretion" in federal procurement. Even when regulations permit flexibility, officials avoid exercising judgment because discretion creates audit risk while conformity provides protection. An official who follows procedures exactly and fails faces no personal consequence; an official who exercises discretion and fails faces investigation. This fear of discretion amplifies technical challenges into extended timelines: rather than making judgment calls to work around obstacles, officials default to the safest interpretation of every requirement, extending timelines at each decision point.

### 2.5 Alternative Explanations

A complete account must consider competing explanations for observed delays.

Contractor incentive structures represent one alternative explanation. Cost-plus contracts, common in federal IT acquisition, may incentivize contractors to extend rather than accelerate timelines. CACI International, IPPS-A's prime contractor, benefits financially from extended development periods. This principal-agent dynamic likely contributes to observed delays. However, we note that contractor incentive structures operate within the institutional framework rather than independent of it: cost-plus contracting is itself an institutional choice driven by the difficulty of specifying complete contracts for complex IT systems under uncertainty—a challenge amplified by institutional constraints that make requirements unstable over multi-year procurement cycles. The contractor incentive explanation is not wrong, but it is incomplete without the institutional context that shapes contracting choices.

Technical complexity differences represent another alternative explanation. Perhaps military HR systems are simply more complex than retail HR systems, and observed delays reflect genuine technical challenges rather than institutional constraints. We address this directly in Section 5, where we establish that Walmart and Army implementations involve comparable technical complexity along measurable dimensions. Military HR involves distinctive complexity in pay structures (combat pay, housing allowances, deployment adjustments); retail HR involves distinctive complexity in transaction volumes, global regulatory fragmentation, and currency management. Neither domain is clearly more complex than the other—they are differently complex. Divergent outcomes cannot be attributed to one organization facing an inherently easier technical challenge.

COVID-19 disruption represents a temporal confound. IPPS-A's Release 3 delay (December 2021 to January 2023) coincided with the pandemic. Three observations mitigate this concern. First, IPPS-A experienced schedule slippage before March 2020, establishing institutional delay patterns preceding COVID-19. Second, if COVID-19 were the primary cause, we would expect comparable delays across public and private implementations during the same period; instead, outcomes continued to diverge by sector. Third, the 36-month payroll integration deferral extends to 2026, well beyond any reasonable pandemic attribution window.

Management quality differences cannot be ruled out. Perhaps Army project management was simply inferior to Walmart's. However, the persistence of similar outcomes across federal agencies with different leadership, different contractors, and different technical requirements suggests institutional rather than managerial causation. If poor management were the primary driver, we would expect substantial variance in outcomes reflecting variance in management quality. Instead, we observe convergence toward similar unfavorable outcomes—the pattern institutional explanations predict.

### 2.6 Why Reform Fails

Understanding this framework explains why decades of federal IT reform have produced limited results:

Pressure fails because it treats symptoms (slow projects) rather than causes (veto player accumulation). Pressure cannot overcome structural gridlock.

Simplification fails because institutions resist it. Every rule exists because someone wanted it; removing rules mobilizes constituencies. FAR "simplification" efforts have consistently added pages, not removed them.

Bypass fails systemically because circumventing constraints creates precedents that erode legitimate purposes constraints originally served.

These constraints were not mistakes. They were solutions to real problems: the spoils system really did produce incompetent government; corrupt procurement really did waste public funds. The instruments created to solve these problems worked. The problem is their transformation into institutions that resist reform—compounded by the absence of survival pressure that might otherwise force organizations through the barriers.

## 3. INSTITUTIONAL CONTEXT OF FEDERAL HR ACQUISITION

### 3.1 Federal Acquisition Regulation

The Federal Acquisition Regulation (FAR) governs procurement for all executive branch agencies. Over 2,000 pages of accumulated regulations implement approximately 40 public laws related to government procurement.

For IT acquisition specifically, FAR imposes procedural requirements that extend timelines relative to private procurement:

Competition Requirements under FAR Part 6 mandate public solicitation, adequate response time, and objective evaluation criteria. These requirements extend procurement timelines by several months relative to direct negotiation, which private organizations can conduct.

Protest Procedures under FAR Part 33 enable disappointed bidders to challenge contract awards. GAO protest resolution requires 100 days; agencies typically suspend performance during this period to avoid the risk of proceeding with a contract that may be overturned. In FY2023, GAO received 2,025 protest filings (GAO, 2024). Each protest represents potential delay; the mere possibility of protest creates risk-averse behavior in source selection.

Sequential Testing requirements mandate completion of System Acceptance Testing (SAT), System Integration Testing (SIT), and Limited User Testing (LUT) phases. Each phase serves as a gatekeeping veto point: failure at any stage halts progress until remediation is complete and verified. While private organizations can compress testing phases or run them concurrently based on risk tolerance, federal organizations must satisfy each gate sequentially.

### 3.2 Appropriations Constraints

Congressional appropriations occur annually, misaligned with IT project cycles that typically span multiple years. Agencies cannot commit to multi-year funding without specific authority. Budget constraints emerge unpredictably when Congress revises appropriations or reallocates funds through continuing resolutions. The Anti-Deficiency Act (31 U.S.C. Section 1341) prohibits obligating funds in excess of appropriations, creating extreme risk aversion around spending acceleration. An official who obligates funds not yet appropriated faces criminal penalties; an official who slows a project to stay within appropriations faces no consequence.

### 3.3 Civil Service and Union Constraints

Federal employees are protected through civil service rules and collective bargaining agreements. Technology changes affecting compensation, work location, or job classification require notification periods, union consultation, grievance procedures, and potentially binding arbitration. Each adds time—protecting workers while extending implementation timelines. These protections exist for good reason: federal workers historically faced politically-motivated dismissals and adverse actions. The cost of these protections is measured in project timelines.

## 4. RESEARCH DESIGN

### 4.1 Comparative Case Study Approach

We employ comparative case study methodology to examine the Institutional Amplification Hypothesis:

    Army IPPS-A (high institutional intensity) -> predicts high drift
    Walmart Workday (low institutional intensity) -> predicts minimal drift
    OPM HCM (moderate institutional intensity) -> predicts moderate drift

The empirical logic is straightforward: if institutional constraints drive Integration Drift, organizations with similar technical complexity but different institutional contexts should exhibit different outcomes.

### 4.2 Case Selection Rationale

IPPS-A was selected as the primary federal case because it represents the largest federal HR system consolidation, has well-documented public delays through GAO and DOT&E reporting, operates under known institutional constraints, and involves technical scope comparable to large private-sector implementations.

Walmart was selected as the private-sector comparison because it involves a comparable employee population (2.1 million versus 1.1 million), comparable legacy system complexity, achieved full retirement within planned timelines, and operates under minimal institutional constraints relative to federal agencies.

OPM's forthcoming government-wide HCM implementation was selected because it provides a prospective test of predictions. Unlike IPPS-A and Walmart, where outcomes are known, OPM allows us to register predictions before observing results.

### 4.3 ITO Decomposition Framework

We analyze delays using the ITO Attribution Framework:

    D_total = D_I + D_T + D_O + (interaction effects)

Where D_I represents institutional delay attributable to constraints documented in statute, regulation, or contract; D_T represents technical delay driven by system complexity observable in comparable private implementations; and D_O represents organizational delay driven by execution choices within management control.

We treat these components as analytically separable while allowing for interaction effects in interpretation. The key test: if institutional constraints merely add to delay, components should be separable and interaction effects should be minimal. If institutional constraints amplify other delays, we should observe interaction effects where institutional factors extend the duration of technical or organizational delays beyond what would be expected in private-sector contexts.

### 4.4 Addressing Confounds

COVID-19 represents a potential confound for IPPS-A timeline analysis. The Release 3 delay from December 2021 to January 2023 overlapped with pandemic disruption. We address this confound through three observations. First, IPPS-A experienced schedule slippage before March 2020, establishing a pattern of institutional delay that preceded COVID-19. Second, if COVID-19 were the primary cause of delays, we would expect comparable delays across public and private implementations during the same period; instead, outcomes continued to diverge by sector during and after the pandemic. Third, the 36-month payroll integration deferral extends to 2026, well beyond any reasonable window for pandemic attribution.

Selection bias represents a methodological limitation. Cases were selected because outcomes were known, which could bias findings toward confirming the hypothesis. We acknowledge this limitation and note that within-sector variance exists: some federal IT projects succeed more quickly than IPPS-A, and some private implementations encounter significant delays. Our claim is not that all federal projects drift while all private projects succeed, but that institutional constraints systematically shift the distribution of outcomes toward longer timelines and incomplete integration.

## 5. CASE ANALYSIS

### 5.1 Case 1: Army IPPS-A

#### 5.1.1 Project Overview

IPPS-A is the Army's effort to consolidate more than 50 legacy HR systems into a single integrated platform serving 1.1 million personnel across Active, Guard, and Reserve components. CACI International was awarded the contract in 2015. Full deployment to all Army components was reported in January 2023, following more than $1 billion in investment over approximately eight years of development.

#### 5.1.2 Documented Timeline and Delays

    Original Release 3 target:     December 2021
    FAR testing issues announced:  October 2021
    Release 3 achieved:            January 2023 (13-month delay)
    Payroll integration deferred:  2023 -> 2026 (36-month deferral)

The October 2021 announcement specifically cited System Acceptance Testing (SAT) issues. Federal acquisition regulations require sequential completion of SAT, SIT, and LUT phases—each a gatekeeping veto point.

#### 5.1.3 ITO Attribution

Institutional factors documented in the public record include FAR sequential testing requirements, which produced approximately 9 months of delay following the October 2021 announcement as the program worked through mandated testing gates. Appropriations constraints contributed to the 36-month payroll integration deferral, as funding alignment across fiscal years proved difficult to achieve. Civil service change management requirements, including the October 2022 pay standardization, added notification and consultation periods that extended implementation timelines.

Technical factors documented in the public record include legacy data quality issues. DOT&E testing documentation reported that 95.2% of test participants found errors in their personnel data—a data quality challenge that required extensive remediation. Interface complexity added additional technical burden, with 15 or more Army and DOD systems feeding data to IPPS-A, each requiring validated interfaces.

The timing of delays provides evidence for institutional rather than purely technical causation. Delays correlate temporally with institutional milestones (FAR testing announcements, appropriations deferrals) rather than solely with technical discovery. Technical problems were identified early; the extended remediation timeline reflects institutional constraints on how quickly technical problems could be addressed.

#### 5.1.4 Amplification Evidence

What is often a months-long data cleanup effort in private implementations became multi-year remediation in this institutional context. The approximately eight-year timeline from 2015 contract award to 2023 deployment substantially exceeds private sector implementations of similar scale. This extended timeline is difficult to attribute solely to technical complexity; private sector implementations of comparable or greater complexity have achieved deployment in substantially shorter timeframes. We interpret this divergence as evidence of institutional amplification—institutional constraints not merely adding to technical delays but extending them through sequential requirements, constraint cascades, and risk-averse responses.

### 5.2 Case 2: Walmart Workday

Walmart selected Workday as its global HR platform, replacing legacy PeopleSoft systems. Implementation scope: 2.1 million employees globally. Outcome: full legacy system retirement achieved within planned timelines, despite broadly comparable technical complexity to IPPS-A.

#### 5.2.1 Establishing Complexity Equivalence

A rigorous comparison requires establishing that Walmart's implementation involved comparable complexity to IPPS-A. Skeptics might assume that retail HR is inherently simpler than military HR, making the comparison invalid. We address this assumption directly by examining complexity across multiple dimensions.

Scale favors Walmart as the more complex case: 2.1 million employees versus 1.1 million personnel represents nearly twice the population requiring migration.

Geographic scope favors Walmart as the more complex case: operations span 28 countries with multiple currencies, languages, and legal systems, compared to IPPS-A's primarily domestic scope with deployed personnel.

Workforce diversity is comparable: IPPS-A serves Active, Reserve, Guard, and civilian personnel with different rules for each; Walmart serves hourly, salaried, distribution, corporate, and international employees with different rules for each.

Pay structure complexity differs but is comparable in aggregate: IPPS-A involves approximately 20 distinct pay types including base pay, Basic Allowance for Housing (BAH), Basic Allowance for Subsistence (BAS), combat pay, hazard duty pay, special duty pay, and numerous other allowances. Walmart involves fewer pay types but manages them across multiple currencies, with international variations in benefits, bonuses, and compensation structures that create equivalent complexity.

Regulatory compliance arguably favors Walmart as the more complex case: IPPS-A operates under military regulations and federal personnel rules—complex, but unified. Walmart operates under 50 different state labor law regimes domestically, plus 28 different national labor law regimes internationally, with varying minimum wages, overtime rules, break requirements, scheduling laws, and tax codes.

Transaction volume favors Walmart as the more complex case: retail turnover often exceeds 100% annually in some job categories, generating massive hire, termination, and transfer transaction volumes. Military turnover is lower, though status changes (deployment, promotion, component transfer) add complexity.

Operational stakes are comparable: military HR errors affect readiness and personnel welfare, creating mission-critical stakes. Retail HR errors affect the livelihoods of 2.1 million employees and their families. Additionally, Walmart operates one of the largest trucking fleets in the United States; transportation and logistics roles have fatality rates exceeding many military occupational specialties. Both organizations face high-stakes consequences for HR system failures.

The conclusion from this assessment: neither implementation is clearly simpler than the other. Complexity is distributed differently across the two organizations but comparable in aggregate. Divergent outcomes cannot be attributed to Walmart facing an inherently easier technical challenge.

#### 5.2.2 Institutional Context Comparison

    Constraint Type      Walmart              Federal Agency
    ----------------------------------------------------------------
    Procurement          Corporate policy     FAR (2,000+ pages)
    Competition req.     None (negotiated)    Full & open
    Protest mechanism    None                 GAO/COFC (100 days)
    Budget cycle         Continuous           Annual appropriations
    Union constraints    Limited              AFGE/NTEU
    Competitive pressure Intense              None
    Budget constraint    Hard (bankruptcy)    Soft (appropriations)

#### 5.2.3 Why the Divergence?

The Kornai framework illuminates the key structural difference. Walmart operates under hard budget constraints and intense competitive pressure. Every dollar spent maintaining legacy systems reduces operating margin. Every month of delayed integration creates competitive disadvantage relative to Target, Amazon, Costco, and other rivals who might modernize faster. The cost of redundancy—maintaining parallel systems—threatens survival if competitors achieve efficiency gains that Walmart foregoes. This creates sustained, unrelenting pressure to retire legacy systems even when doing so entails transition risk. The CEO who delays integration to avoid transition risk may lose the company to competitors who accept that risk and succeed.

The Army operates under soft budget constraints and faces no competitive pressure. Legacy maintenance costs are absorbed by appropriations; they do not threaten organizational survival because no competitor can capture market share. No rival army will gain advantage if the U.S. Army integrates its HR systems slowly. But a failed cutover creates immediate, concentrated, attributable consequences: a soldier not paid, a benefit miscalculated, a congressional hearing convened, a career ended. Under these conditions, rational managers preserve legacy systems indefinitely. The financial cost of redundancy is diffuse and invisible; the political cost of transition failure is concentrated and career-ending.

Both organizations respond rationally to their incentive structures. The Army is not poorly managed; it is differently constrained. Walmart is not better led; it faces different survival pressures. The primary difference is structural rather than managerial.

### 5.3 Case 3: OPM Government-Wide HCM

OPM is launching a government-wide HR system consolidation initiative targeting October 2025. This case provides prospective test of predictions.

#### 5.3.1 Predictions (Sealed)

The following predictions are registered as of December 2025, prior to OPM HCM contract award:

    PREDICTION 1: Award timeline will exceed RFP schedule by 6-12 months.

        Registration date:        December 2025
        Evaluation date:          18 months post-RFP
        Falsification criterion:  Award within 3 months of RFP schedule

        If award occurs within 3 months of schedule, this prediction fails
        and the Institutional Amplification Hypothesis requires revision.

    PREDICTION 2: Agency adoption will reach less than 60% at T+24 months.

        Registration date:        December 2025
        Evaluation date:          24 months post-award
        Falsification criterion:  Greater than 75% agency participation

        If adoption exceeds 75% at 24 months, this prediction fails.

## 6. DISCUSSION

### 6.1 Theoretical Implications

We extend M&A integration research by identifying Integration Drift as an institutional contingency on classic M&A integration theory—conditions under which integration strategy diverges from integration outcome:

    Realized Integration Mode = f(Intended Mode, Institutional Constraints)

Federal agencies do not choose preservation; they are pushed toward it by veto player gridlock and face no survival pressure (under soft budget constraints) to push back.

### 6.2 The Counterfactual

Would the Army achieve Walmart-like outcomes with Walmart-like institutional freedom? We must be careful with this question. The Army cannot and should not operate without democratic oversight; that oversight is not a bug to be eliminated but a feature of legitimate governance. The question is not whether to remove constraints but whether the current configuration produces unnecessary amplification beyond what democratic accountability requires.

Available evidence is consistent with an affirmative answer to the narrower question: could the Army integrate faster under different institutional arrangements? Technical capability exists—federal contractors succeed in private implementations using the same technologies. Resources were adequate—over $1 billion was invested. Technical complexity was comparable to successful private implementations. What distinguishes IPPS-A from Walmart is documented institutional constraints: FAR testing serialization, appropriations cycle misalignment, union consultation requirements. These are features of the institutional environment, not failures of management or technology.

### 6.3 Policy Implications

The unified framework enables diagnostic clarity. To be explicit: this analysis does not argue for eliminating democratic safeguards; it argues for distinguishing necessary constraints from unnecessary amplification.

Some constraints serve democratic values and should be retained. Transparency in procurement prevents corruption and should be preserved. Union consultation protects workers and should be preserved. Appropriations oversight ensures accountability and should be preserved. Protest mechanisms ensure fair competition and should be preserved.

Some constraints may be institutional artifacts rather than democratic necessities and warrant examination. Sequential testing requirements may be mandated by statute or may simply reflect agency habit; distinguishing statutory requirements from customary practice could reveal flexibility. Fiscal year boundary effects that fragment multi-year IT projects might be addressable through multi-year appropriations authority without sacrificing accountability.

Some constraints appear to produce unnecessary amplification and represent candidates for reform. Procurement culture risk-aversion—Kelman's "fear of discretion"—extends timelines even when regulations would permit faster action. Cascading approval gates that serialize inherently parallel activities could potentially be consolidated while preserving legitimate oversight purposes.

### 6.4 Implications for OPM Implementation

If our framework is correct, OPM's forthcoming implementation will encounter predictable challenges regardless of management quality or stated intentions. The institutional constraints that produced IPPS-A outcomes remain in place for OPM. Policymakers seeking different outcomes might consider structural interventions: securing multi-year appropriations authority to reduce fiscal year boundary effects; consolidating testing gates where sequential requirements are customary rather than statutory; front-loading union consultation to parallelize rather than serialize constraint satisfaction; establishing clear authority for decision-makers to say "yes" rather than only mechanisms for stakeholders to say "no."

### 6.5 Limitations

Three cases cannot support population-level claims. We offer existence proof, not prevalence estimates. The finding that institutional constraints produced Integration Drift in IPPS-A does not establish that institutional constraints always produce drift or that drift never occurs in private implementations.

Precise quantification of ITO components requires internal program documentation not publicly available. Our attribution estimates rely on public sources—GAO reports, DOT&E documentation, press releases, budget documents—which provide temporal correlation but not definitive causal attribution.

OPM predictions are speculative and will be evaluated against outcomes we cannot yet observe. Prediction failure would require theoretical revision; prediction success would provide supporting evidence but not definitive confirmation.

---

## 7. CONCLUSION

Public sector IT projects take longer than private sector equivalents. This is an empirical regularity. The question is why—and what follows from the answer.

The unified framework provides a structured explanation: institutional constraints that were once instruments solving real problems have become institutions serving their own perpetuation. These constraints accumulate, resist reform, and amplify project timelines. And because public organizations operate under soft budget constraints, they face no survival pressure to overcome these barriers.

This does not excuse outcomes. It enables intelligent response. Understanding that Integration Drift is a predictable institutional outcome—not management failure—enables policymakers to distinguish which delays are necessary costs of democratic governance and which represent unnecessary amplification.

IPPS-A is better understood as the outcome of an organization navigating institutional constraints it cannot unilaterally change—and lacking the survival pressure to force its way through. OPM is about to attempt the same thing. Making predictions public creates accountability: if OPM follows IPPS-A patterns, policymakers should ask not "why did this project fail?" but "what institutional reforms would enable different outcomes?"

## APPENDIX A: ITO ATTRIBUTION CODING PROTOCOL

STEP 1: Identify delay episode from documentary source

STEP 2: Determine primary cause category using decision tree

STEP 3: Validate classification using secondary evidence

STEP 4: Estimate baseline duration from comparable private implementation

STEP 5: Code interaction effects where applicable

Decision Tree:

    Is delay caused by statute, regulation, contract, or constitutional
    requirement that organization cannot change?
        -> YES: Code as INSTITUTIONAL (I)
        -> NO: Continue

    Is delay caused by system characteristics (legacy architecture, data
    quality, interface complexity) independent of institutional context?
        -> YES: Code as TECHNICAL (T)
        -> NO: Continue

    Is delay caused by planning decisions, resource allocation, or execution
    choices within organizational control?
        -> YES: Code as ORGANIZATIONAL (O)
        -> NO: Review and reclassify

Amplification Assessment:

    Did institutional constraints extend the duration of technical
    remediation beyond private-sector baseline for equivalent challenge?
        -> YES: Code interaction effect (I × T)
        -> Estimate amplification factor: (Actual duration) / (Baseline duration)
