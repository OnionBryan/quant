Part I

Introduction

The Atlanta Puzzle
Context & Motivation
  • The Paradox: Atlanta is a top-tier U.S. city with Fortune 500 headquarters, world-class universities (Georgia Tech), and a thriving logistics hub—yet founders struggle to secure investor meetings in ways that San Francisco or Boston founders do not.
  • The Structural Deficit: San Francisco has approximately 12 venture capital firms per 100,000 residents; Atlanta has less than 1 (Sorenson & Stuart, 2001). This is a 15-to-1 deficit.
  • The Consequence: Many talented entrepreneurs—with solid ideas, capable teams, and real traction—spend months waiting for investor callbacks. Similar ventures in coastal cities move quickly to funding.
  • The Central Claim: In low-access capital markets like Atlanta, knowing which intermediaries to use and how to use them matters as much as the quality of the business idea itself.

Research Questions
Two Practical Questions for Low-Capital Markets

Research Question 1: The Founder Strategy

How should founders in Atlanta structure their intermediary portfolio strategy to get investor meetings quickly and secure funding?

Research Question 2: The Funder Perspective

What do investors actually see as the barriers to deploying capital in Atlanta and similar markets?

Key Definitions:

  • Low-Access Capital Market: A region where VC firm density is low, geographic distance creates information gaps, and companies must rely on intermediaries rather than direct connections (Sorenson & Stuart, 2001; Chen et al., 2010).
  • Intermediary: Any person or organization that connects founders to funders—accelerators (Techstars), angel groups, mentors, university programs, corporate innovation hubs.
  • Early-Stage Startups: Seed or Series A ventures seeking first major institutional investment ($500K-$5M) with working product and initial traction.

Part II

Literature Review

Theoretical Foundation: Network Theory
Structural Holes & Weak Ties

Core Theory: Structural Holes (Burt, 1992)

Founders and investors exist in separate social networks. The gap between them is a "structural hole"—an opportunity for intermediaries to bridge disconnected groups. Entrepreneurs who span these holes gain access to non-redundant information and resources.

Supporting Theory: Weak Ties (Granovetter, 1973)

Access to distant, diverse contacts ("weak ties") provides more novel information than close, redundant ties. Intermediaries facilitate these weak ties by connecting founders to investors outside their immediate network.

Empirical Evidence (Vissa, 2012)

Broker diversity improves entrepreneurial outcomes. Founders who strategically build relationships across multiple intermediary types gain access to more and better opportunities than those relying on a single channel.

Gap #1: Existing research conflates variety (richness—how many types) with balance (evenness—how equally distributed). No study quantifies concentration risk using the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI) in this context.

Broker Intermediation Literature
Catalyzing Ties & Adverse Selection

Core Theory: Catalyzing Ties (Hallen & Eisenhardt, 2012)

Instead of cold-emailing VCs, founders leverage intermediaries to provide "warm introductions"—these catalyzing ties dramatically accelerate access. Intermediaries reduce information asymmetry by vouching for founder quality.

The Dark Side: Adverse Selection (Yimfor, 2021, 2022)

Investors fear that only low-quality ventures seek out-of-region funding or use weak intermediaries. This creates a sorting problem: founders using lower-tier brokers may be perceived as worse-quality deals, even if untrue.

Empirical Finding: Timing Matters

Brokers reduce time-to-funding by approximately 3.2 months on average. However, broker quality varies significantly by type (accelerators vs. mentors vs. angel groups).

Gap #2: Research treats brokers as homogeneous. Broker tier effects (national accelerators like Y Combinator vs. regional vs. informal mentors) remain empirically untested. We don't know whether elite broker access is determined by founder credentials or founder preparedness.

Resource Dependence Theory
The Concentration Trap

Core Theory (Pfeffer & Salancik, 1978)

When an organization relies on a single, critical supplier for a vital resource, it faces "hold-up risk" and loses bargaining power. Dependence on one resource channel creates strategic vulnerability.

Application: Power Imbalance (Casciaro & Piskorski, 2005)

Concentrated resource dependencies create power imbalances. If a founder's sole path to investors is through one accelerator, and that accelerator's network is misaligned with the founder's sector, the founder is stuck with no alternatives.

The Concentration Trap

Many founders make a critical mistake: they find one good intermediary (a mentor who knows several VCs) and put all their energy into that one relationship. This creates hidden risk. What if that mentor's network isn't the best fit? What if they stop helping?

Gap #3: No empirical study has quantified broker concentration risk in the capital access context. We propose using the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI)—a standard measure of market concentration—to measure dependency risk in founder intermediary portfolios.

Absorptive Capacity Theory
The Missing Piece: Can You Execute?

Core Theory (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990)

It's not enough for a firm to access external knowledge—the firm must possess the absorptive capacity to recognize, assimilate, and apply it. Access without capability is wasted opportunity.

Application: Founder Preparedness

An intermediary gets you the meeting. But once you're sitting across from the investor, the founder has to execute. If the investor asks, "What's your burn rate?" or "What's your customer acquisition cost?" and the founder can't answer, the meeting is over. The great introduction is wasted.

Deliberate Practice (Ericsson et al., 2006)

Pitching, financial literacy, and business model clarity are skills developed through deliberate practice. First-time pitchers ramble; veterans are clear and compelling. Preparedness amplifies the value of network access.

Gap #4: Network research emphasizes access but ignores whether founders can convert access into funding. We don't know if preparedness moderates the relationship between intermediary strategy and outcomes—yet this could explain why identical networks yield different results.

Integrated Research Gap
What Has Not Been Done

The Four Gaps in the Literature:

  1. Network theory conflates variety with balance—no HHI quantification
  2. Broker research treats intermediaries as homogeneous—tier effects untested
  3. Resource dependence lacks empirical metrics for concentration risk
  4. Absorptive capacity is ignored in network access literature

What This Study Proposes to Do:

We will empirically test how broker portfolio configuration (richness, evenness, HHI, volume), broker tier access determinants (legitimacy vs. capability pathways), and founder preparedness (conversion ability) interact to shape capital access speed and funding outcomes in low-capital markets.

This integrates four major theories—network theory (Burt, 1992), broker intermediation (Hallen & Eisenhardt, 2012), Resource Dependence Theory (Pfeffer & Salancik, 1978), and absorptive capacity (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990)—into a unified, testable framework.

Part III

Methodology

Research Design Overview
Mixed-Methods Approach

Sample: 200 Early-Stage Tech Founders

  • Atlanta: N = 100 (low VC density: <1 firm per 100K residents)
  • Austin: N = 100 (moderate VC density for comparison)
  • Stage: Pre-seed through Series A (seeking $500K-$5M)
  • Status: Mix of successful and failed fundraises (not just success stories)

Recruitment Strategy: Multi-Channel

  • Primary: Partner with intermediaries (ATDC, Techstars Atlanta, Capital Factory, angel groups) to distribute surveys to current/recent cohorts
  • Secondary: Targeted outreach via LinkedIn and Crunchbase to identify founders who fit criteria but may not have used formal intermediaries
  • Tertiary: Snowball sampling through interviews

Qualitative Component: Investor Interviews

  • N = 20+ active investors (VCs and angels)
  • Semi-structured interviews exploring risk perception, signal value, market quality, and sector focus
  • Thematic analysis to test H3 (Signal-and-Search Hypothesis)
Statistical Power: N=200 provides adequate power (β = 0.80) to detect medium effect sizes (Cohen's d = 0.50) in regression models.

Research Hypotheses
Four Testable Predictions
  • H1a (Wide Net Hypothesis): Founders who use a greater number of distinct intermediary types (high richness) will secure investor meetings faster and have greater funding success than founders who use fewer types.
  • H1b (Concentration Trap Hypothesis): Founders whose intermediary introductions are highly concentrated in a single type (high HHI) will take longer to secure meetings and have lower funding success.
  • H2a/b (Preparedness Moderation Hypothesis): The success of a founder's intermediary strategy is moderated by their preparedness. The positive effects of a diverse portfolio (H1a) and the negative effects of a concentrated one (H1b) will be strongest for founders who are highly prepared.
  • H3 (Signal-and-Search Hypothesis): Investors evaluating Atlanta deals will identify challenges related to (a) validating company quality from a distance (signaling) and (b) the time and cost required to source and vet deals (search costs) as significant barriers to investment.
Note: H1a, H1b, and H2 will be tested quantitatively via regression models. H3 will be tested qualitatively via thematic analysis of investor interviews.

Variables & Measurement
How We Operationalize the Constructs
Construct Operationalization Example Question
Richness (IV) Simple count of intermediary types used (1-5)
Types: Accelerators, Angel Groups, Mentors, Universities, Corporate		 "Which types of intermediaries did you use to get investor introductions?"
HHI (IV) Herfindahl-Hirschman Index:
HHI = Σ(share of introductions from type i)²
Range: 0.20 (balanced) to 1.0 (all from one type)		 "How many investor introductions came from each intermediary type?"
Preparedness (Moderator) Composite score from:
1. Financial Literacy Quiz (3 questions)
2. Pitch Experience (# of pitches delivered)
3. Business Model Clarity (1-3 researcher rating)		 "A VC asks for your 'runway.' What two key numbers do you need to calculate this?"
Weeks to Meeting (DV) Count variable: weeks from first investor contact to first meaningful meeting (20+ min call) "How many weeks from initial outreach to your first substantive investor conversation?"
Funding Success (DV) Binary (1/0): Did founder successfully raise capital? "Did you successfully close a funding round? (Yes/No)"
Controls Insider Status, Sector, Stage, Market (ATL/AUS), Team Size "Are you a serial founder? Did you attend an elite university?"

Quantitative Analysis Plan
Two-Phase Regression Approach

Phase 1: Main Effects Models (H1a & H1b)

Model 1 (Access Speed):

  • DV: Weeks_to_Meeting (count data)
  • Method: Negative Binomial Regression (preferred over Poisson for over-dispersed count data)
  • IVs: Richness, HHI
  • Controls: Insider Status, Sector, Stage, Market (ATL/AUS), Team Size
  • Prediction: Richness will have negative coefficient (faster access), HHI will have positive coefficient (slower access)

Model 2 (Funding Success):

  • DV: Raised_Capital (binary: 1/0)
  • Method: Logistic Regression
  • IVs: Richness, HHI
  • Controls: Same as Model 1
  • Prediction: Richness will increase odds of funding, HHI will decrease odds

Phase 2: Moderation Analysis (H2)

  • Hierarchical approach: Add Preparedness score and interaction terms (Richness × Preparedness, HHI × Preparedness) to Models 1 & 2
  • Interpretation: Significant interaction term supports H2 (preparedness amplifies portfolio effects)
  • Visualization: Plot simple slopes at high/low preparedness to show moderation
Qualitative Analysis (H3): Thematic coding of 20+ investor interviews to identify emergent themes related to signaling barriers, search costs, local market risk, and talent perception. Themes will be categorized and validated against H3.
