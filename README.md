# How Early-Stage Ventures Break Through Capital Barriers

**DBA Research Project - Dr. Nargundkar**
**November 2025**

## Overview

This repository contains research on how early-stage ventures in low-access capital markets (like Atlanta) can break through capital barriers by strategically leveraging intermediaries. The study investigates why intermediaries matter in markets with structural venture capital deficits.

## Research Focus

### The Atlanta Puzzle

Atlanta represents a paradox in venture capital:
- Top-tier U.S. city with Fortune 500 headquarters and world-class universities
- **15-to-1 venture capital deficit** compared to San Francisco
- Talented entrepreneurs with solid ideas struggle to secure investor meetings
- Geographic and structural barriers create information gaps between founders and funders

### Core Research Questions

1. **Founder Strategy**: How should founders in Atlanta structure their intermediary portfolio strategy to get investor meetings quickly and secure funding?

2. **Funder Perspective**: What do investors actually see as the barriers to deploying capital in Atlanta and similar markets?

## Theoretical Framework

This research integrates four major theories:

- **Network Theory** (Burt, 1992): Structural holes and the role of intermediaries in bridging disconnected networks
- **Broker Intermediation** (Hallen & Eisenhardt, 2012): How "warm introductions" catalyze ties and reduce information asymmetry
- **Resource Dependence Theory** (Pfeffer & Salancik, 1978): Concentration risk and power imbalances in resource access
- **Absorptive Capacity** (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990): The ability to recognize, assimilate, and apply external knowledge

## Key Hypotheses

- **H1a (Wide Net Hypothesis)**: Greater intermediary diversity leads to faster investor meetings and higher funding success
- **H1b (Concentration Trap Hypothesis)**: High concentration in a single intermediary type slows access and reduces success
- **H2 (Preparedness Moderation Hypothesis)**: Founder preparedness amplifies the effects of intermediary strategy
- **H3 (Signal-and-Search Hypothesis)**: Investors face signaling and search cost barriers in low-access markets

## Methodology

### Sample

- **200 Early-Stage Tech Founders**
  - 100 from Atlanta (low VC density)
  - 100 from Austin (moderate VC density)
  - Pre-seed through Series A stages ($500K-$5M)
  - Mix of successful and failed fundraises

### Approach

- **Quantitative Analysis**: Regression models testing main effects and moderation
  - Negative Binomial Regression for time-to-meeting
  - Logistic Regression for funding success
  - Moderation analysis for preparedness effects

- **Qualitative Analysis**: Semi-structured interviews with 20+ investors
  - Thematic analysis of risk perception and market barriers
  - Signal value and search cost identification

## Key Variables

| Variable | Description |
|----------|-------------|
| **Richness** | Count of distinct intermediary types used (1-5) |
| **HHI (Concentration)** | Herfindahl-Hirschman Index measuring portfolio concentration |
| **Preparedness** | Composite score: financial literacy + pitch experience + business model clarity |
| **Weeks to Meeting** | Time from first contact to substantive investor conversation |
| **Funding Success** | Binary outcome: successfully raised capital (Yes/No) |

## Key Definitions

- **Low-Access Capital Market**: A region where VC firm density is low, geographic distance creates information gaps, and companies must rely on intermediaries rather than direct connections

- **Intermediary**: Any person or organization that connects founders to funders—accelerators (Techstars), angel groups, mentors, university programs, corporate innovation hubs

- **Early-Stage Startups**: Seed or Series A ventures seeking first major institutional investment ($500K-$5M) with working product and initial traction

## Research Gaps Addressed

1. Network theory conflates variety with balance—no HHI quantification exists
2. Broker research treats intermediaries as homogeneous—tier effects untested
3. Resource dependence lacks empirical metrics for concentration risk
4. Absorptive capacity is ignored in network access literature
